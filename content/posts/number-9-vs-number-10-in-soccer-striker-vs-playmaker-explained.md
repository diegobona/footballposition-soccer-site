## **Quick Answer**

A Number 9 is the striker who plays closest to goal. He finishes chances, pins centre-backs, attacks crosses, and gives the team a target in the box.
A Number 10 is the playmaker behind the striker. He receives between midfield and defence, creates the final pass, and links the attack together.
Simple rule: the Number 9 finishes the move. The Number 10 makes the move happen.

| **Role** | **Number 9** | **Number 10** |
| ---- | -------- | --------- |
| **Main job** | Score goals | Create chances |
| **Usual position** | Striker / centre-forward | Attacking midfielder |
| **Main zone** | Penalty box | Between the lines |
| **Best action** | Shot or box run | Final pass or through ball |
| **Typical examples** | Haaland, Lewandowski, Kane | De Bruyne, Zidane, Bruno Fernandes |

## **What a Number 9 Actually Does**

The Number 9 is the forward defenders worry about first. He stands high, makes centre-backs look over their shoulders, and attacks the box before the ball arrives.
Erling Haaland is the cleanest modern example. He does not need 80 touches. He needs one run across a defender, one pass into the box, and one finish. Robert Lewandowski is a more complete version: he can finish first time, hold the ball, and combine before attacking the six-yard box.
Harry Kane is different. He is still a Number 9, but he has playmaker habits. He drops off, draws a centre-back out, and releases runners. That is why Son Heung-min at Tottenham and England runners around Kane often benefited from his passing.
Old-school examples are easier to understand: Ronaldo Nazario attacked space and beat defenders; Alan Shearer lived on crosses, rebounds, and box positioning. Different styles, same job: lead the line and score.

## **What a Number 10 Actually Does**

The Number 10 is not judged only by goals. He is judged by whether the attack becomes clearer when he gets the ball.
Kevin De Bruyne is a modern Number 10 even when he starts deeper. He finds the early pass, the cut-back, the diagonal ball, or the through ball before the defence is set. Bruno Fernandes is more direct: he risks possession, but he constantly looks for the killer pass.
Classic Number 10s were easier to spot. Zinedine Zidane received under pressure and turned midfield control into attacks. Mesut Ozil lived between the lines and waited for the runner. Juan Roman Riquelme controlled tempo almost like a chess player.
A good Number 10 gives the striker better shots. A great Number 10 changes where the whole defence has to stand.

## **Real Examples: Kane vs De Bruyne**

Kane and De Bruyne show the difference better than a definition.
Kane starts as the striker. He can drop into midfield, but the defence still treats him as the main finishing threat. If he receives near the box, defenders protect the goal first.
De Bruyne starts as the creator. He can arrive in the box and score, but his first danger is the pass. When he receives between the lines or in the right half-space, defenders worry about Haaland, the winger, and the cut-back all at once.
That is the difference: Kane can create from a striker role; De Bruyne can score from a creator role. But their starting responsibilities are not the same.

## **How It Looks in Formations**

In a 4-2-3-1, the difference is obvious: the 9 plays up front, the 10 plays behind him. Think of Kane ahead of an attacking midfielder, or Lewandowski with a creator close to him.
In a 4-3-3, the 10 is often hidden. There may be no fixed attacking midfielder. The creative work can come from an advanced 8, an inverted winger, or a false 9. De Bruyne at Manchester City is a good example: he may start as a central midfielder, but many of his actions are pure Number 10 actions.
This is why shirt numbers can mislead. The real question is not what number the player wears. It is where he receives, where he moves, and what problem he gives the defence.

## **Number 9, False 9 and Number 10**

A normal Number 9 stays high and gives the team a target. Haaland is the easy example.
A False 9 starts as the striker but drops into midfield to drag centre-backs out. Messi under Pep Guardiola and Roberto Firmino at Liverpool are the usual examples.
A Number 10 starts behind the striker and creates from the space between midfield and defence. De Bruyne, Ozil, Zidane, Riquelme and Bruno Fernandes all fit this idea in different ways.

## **Short FAQ**

Is a Number 9 always a striker? Usually yes. The Number 9 is traditionally the central striker or centre-forward.
Is a Number 10 always an attacking midfielder? Usually yes, but modern teams may spread the creative role across an advanced 8, winger or false 9.
Is Messi a 9 or a 10? Messi has been a winger, a Number 10 and a false 9. His role changed with the system.
Is Kane a 9 or a 10? Kane is a Number 9 with strong Number 10 qualities. He leads the line, but he also drops to pass.
What is the easiest way to remember it? Number 9 finishes. Number 10 creates.

## **Bottom Line**

The Number 9 is the striker. He attacks the goal and finishes chances.
The Number 10 is the playmaker. He finds space, links the attack, and creates the final pass.
Modern players can mix both roles, but the basic difference still holds: the 9 is the main finisher; the 10 is the main creator.
