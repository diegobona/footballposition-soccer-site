At the 1998 World Cup, defending champions Brazil were the biggest favorites to win the title. They had the player widely regarded at the time as the best striker in the world: Ronaldo.

### In 1998, Ronaldo was universally recognized as the best forward in world football. 

He combined speed and technique, was extremely efficient in front of goal, and often dribbled past opponents and scored in ways that seemed almost unimaginable.

In 1996, while playing for Barcelona, Ronaldo scored a famous goal in a 5–1 away win over Compostela in La Liga. He dribbled past several defenders in succession, and even when opponents tried to pull him back with their hands, they still could not stop him. After seeing that incredible goal, Barcelona head coach Bobby Robson exclaimed, “It is hard to imagine such a goal being scored by someone from Earth.” From then on, Ronaldo gained the nickname “The Phenomenon,” or “The Alien.”

### That goal for Barcelona helped Ronaldo earn the nickname “The Alien.”

In 1997, Ronaldo joined Inter Milan in Italy for a then-world-record transfer fee of 32 million dollars. At the time, he was only 21 years old. In his first season at Inter, he scored 34 goals in 44 appearances and won both the 1997 FIFA World Player of the Year award and the Ballon d’Or.

By 1998, the 22-year-old striker had become Brazil’s trump card at the World Cup.

In fact, Ronaldo had already been part of Brazil’s squad at the 1994 World Cup. But he was only 18 then, mainly there to learn from his senior teammates and experience the atmosphere. He did not play a single match. Although Brazil eventually won that tournament, the title had little to do with him personally.

This was Ronaldo’s second World Cup, and his role was completely different from four years earlier. He was now Brazil’s biggest star and most reliable goal scorer.

By contrast, host nation France were determined to compete for the title on home soil, but they were not considered the strongest favorites.

Originally, France’s midfield leader was supposed to be Eric Cantona of Manchester United. But in a Premier League match in 1995, Cantona clashed with a Crystal Palace fan and launched a violent kung-fu-style kick at him. As a result, he was banned for eight months.

The incident had an extremely negative impact. During his suspension, Cantona missed a long period of tactical preparation with the national team. In addition, his personality was already rebellious and difficult to manage. France head coach Aimé Jacquet took the opportunity to remove him from the national-team plans.

After that, Jacquet established Zinedine Zidane, who had Algerian roots, as the core of France’s midfield.

### Cantona’s kick at the fan effectively ended his national-team career.

Zidane first joined the French national team in 1994. His debut came in a friendly against the Czech Republic. He came off the bench and scored twice, helping France come back from 2–0 down to draw the match.

He also represented France as a key player at Euro 1996. France did not win that tournament and were eliminated in the semifinals. Zidane’s performances were solid but not spectacular.

However, Jacquet always trusted Zidane deeply. At the 1998 World Cup, Zidane remained the team’s playmaking core.

In the early stages of the tournament, Zidane had both good moments and flaws. He provided two assists in the group stage, but he was also shown a red card against Saudi Arabia for stamping on an opponent.

After advancing from the group, France beat Paraguay 1–0 in the round of 16, eliminated Italy on penalties in the quarterfinals, and then defeated Croatia 2–1 in the semifinals to reach the final.

But in all of those matches, Zidane had not scored a single goal.

### Before the final, Zidane had not scored at the 1998 World Cup.

Compared with him, Ronaldo’s performances for Brazil had been much more eye-catching. As Brazil made their way to the final, Ronaldo contributed four goals and three assists. In the crucial semifinal against the Netherlands, he scored a goal and then took and converted Brazil’s first penalty in the shootout, helping Brazil eliminate the Dutch and set up a final against France.

On the evening of July 12, 1998, the World Cup final was to be played at the Stade de France in Saint-Denis.

At noon that day, after lunch, Ronaldo returned to his hotel room. His roommate was Brazil left-back Roberto Carlos. At that moment, Christian Karembeu of France called Carlos. Karembeu was Carlos’s teammate at Real Madrid, so he wanted to chat with him before the final.

While Carlos was on the phone, Ronaldo was lying on the bed watching Formula One on television.

Then, suddenly, Ronaldo began to convulse and lost consciousness.

Carlos sensed something was wrong. He hurriedly told Karembeu, “I have to hang up,” then ended the call and immediately went to check on Ronaldo.

He saw Ronaldo’s body shaking and foam coming from his mouth. Carlos was frightened and shouted for help. Teammates in nearby rooms rushed over.

Forward Edmundo was the first to arrive, followed by defensive midfielder César Sampaio. Sampaio pulled Ronaldo’s tongue out to prevent him from swallowing it.

When team doctor Lídio Toledo arrived, he saw a terrifying scene: Ronaldo was breathing heavily, with saliva coming from the corner of his mouth. Carlos told him, “It might be epilepsy.”

### Carlos was Ronaldo’s roommate when the episode happened.

After a while, Ronaldo regained consciousness. He had no memory of what had just happened and had no idea what was wrong with him.

His teammate Leonardo accompanied him for a walk in the hotel garden and explained what had happened. Ronaldo also learned that the head coach would not allow him to play in the World Cup final.

Later reports claimed that Brazil’s technical adviser Zico opposed letting Ronaldo play, and some players led by Leonardo agreed. But another group of players, led by Dunga, hoped Ronaldo could still play. In their view, even if Ronaldo was not in good physical condition, he was still indispensable.

Ronaldo was taken to hospital, where doctors performed neurological examinations. The whole process lasted three hours, but the specific cause of his episode remained unclear.

Doctors found no physical abnormality. Brazil team doctor Toledo also found nothing wrong and believed Ronaldo was fully able to play.

So what exactly had happened?

Many years later, cardiologist Bruno Caru suggested: “Ronaldo was lying in bed watching Formula One. A movement of his neck compressed the carotid body, which regulates heart rate and arterial blood pressure. His heart rate dropped, and that caused him to faint and convulse.”

However, Roberto Carlos’s remark that it “might be epilepsy” seems to have led doctors toward a mistaken judgment. They treated it as an epileptic seizure and gave Ronaldo phenobarbital, a powerful anti-epileptic drug.

This medication reduces brain activity and later triggered a series of consequences.

Brazilian players were usually optimistic. Part of this came from their personalities, but more importantly, it came from their confidence in their own strength. In the past, when the team traveled to a match venue, they would laugh, chat, and play music.

But Ronaldo’s sudden illness made the whole team atmosphere heavy. When the other players left for the stadium, Ronaldo had not even returned from the hospital.

Many players did not know exactly what had happened. They had only heard that “Ronaldo is not feeling well; he went to the hospital.”

Everyone had an ominous feeling about the match. The whole team was anxious, and the bus was completely silent. No one played music. No one laughed or talked.

British commentator John Motson was responsible for broadcasting the World Cup final to listeners around the world. When he received the starting lineups, he was shocked to find that Ronaldo’s name was not on the list. His starting place had been taken by Edmundo.

Motson and his colleagues even wondered whether they had misread it. None of them understood what had happened.

Motson quickly asked his colleague Stubbs to contact Brazilian legend Pelé and find out what was going on. After asking, the answer came back: Pelé did not know either.

### Brazil had originally planned to start Edmundo in place of Ronaldo.

It was like a concert about to begin with the lead singer absent. The commentators were anxious and confused until they received a revised team sheet.

This time, Ronaldo’s name appeared again. He replaced Edmundo, who had originally been listed as the starter.

The commentators were even more confused. Although Ronaldo’s name had been put back on the list, no one knew whether he would actually appear on the pitch.

Football journalists around the world had different opinions about the matter. Some even floated conspiracy theories, saying Brazil were using the trick to disturb France’s mentality.

At the same time, in the French dressing room, the players also believed Brazil were playing mind games.

Defender Lilian Thuram said, “Not letting Ronaldo play? Impossible! They are trying to fool us with this tactic.”

But it was clear that something really had gone wrong with Brazil. Before the match, they were even late coming out to warm up.

Motson later recalled: “Clearly, something had happened in Brazil’s dressing room, but we did not know what.”

So what exactly happened inside Brazil’s dressing room?

After brief treatment, Ronaldo arrived at Brazil’s dressing room. He made it clear to head coach Mário Zagallo that he wanted to play the final.

“I’m fine. I don’t feel anything wrong.”

As he said this, he handed over the medical results.

“The tests say there is no problem. I want to play.”

### Ronaldo and Zagallo.

Ronaldo’s attitude was firm. He later recalled: “I did not give the coach any other choice. He had to accept my decision.”

His teammates were delighted, and their confidence in the final rose significantly.

Goalkeeper Dida recalled: “We all knew that Ronaldo could do anything in a match.”

Because of Ronaldo’s insistence, Zagallo made the decision to change the starting lineup again. After all, when a superstar of that level believed he could play, it was almost impossible for the coach to stop him.

But this change created another problem. In the hours before the match, Zagallo had hastily drawn up a new game plan for Ronaldo’s absence. Once Ronaldo returned, those newly made plans were scrapped.

On the French side, however, the preparations had been thorough.

Before the match, Jacquet had told the team that Brazil were very vulnerable when defending corners. As long as France delivered the ball well from corner kicks, there would be a good chance to score.

The match unfolded exactly as he had predicted.

In the 27th minute, France won a corner on the left. Midfielder Emmanuel Petit curled in an inswinging delivery and found Zidane, who had not scored earlier in the tournament.

The 1.85-meter-tall Zidane rose high and headed the ball into Brazil’s net.

In first-half stoppage time, France won another corner, this time from the right. Youri Djorkaeff sent the ball in, and once again it was Zidane who reached it first. The ball went between Roberto Carlos’s legs and flew into the net.

France led 2–0.

### Zidane scored twice with headers.

In fact, although Zidane was tall, he was not known for his heading ability. He scored very few headed goals in his entire career. But in this final, it was as if he had been touched by divine inspiration. He scored twice against Brazil in the same way.

France were now in a very favorable position.

But in the 68th minute of the second half, the situation suddenly changed. France center-back Marcel Desailly received his second yellow card of the match and was sent off.

France had to hold on for more than 20 minutes with one man fewer, which brought suspense back into the game.

Brazil quickly brought on forward Edmundo in an attempt to strengthen their attack.

France responded immediately by bringing on defensive midfielder Patrick Vieira to add more toughness in midfield.

After Desailly was sent off, French midfielder Petit dropped back into the defensive line and partnered Frank Leboeuf at center-back.

As France defended stubbornly, time slowly passed. With five minutes left, the result was almost settled. The crowd was already celebrating, waiting for France to be crowned champions.

Petit could no longer suppress his excitement. He shouted to Leboeuf: “My dream has come true!”

Leboeuf did not join in. Instead, he cut him off: “What are you talking about? Focus on the match!”

Petit had a reason for saying that.

When he was a child, he had written a sentence: “One day, we will beat Brazil 2–0 at home and win the World Cup final.”

At the time he wrote those words, France had not even been awarded the right to host the World Cup.

Now France were leading 2–0, and the match was about to end. Petit felt as if his childhood dream had become reality.

Then the match entered its final moments, and France had a chance to counterattack.

Patrick Vieira, Petit’s teammate at Arsenal, played a beautiful pass to him.

Petit sprinted forward at high speed and calmly lifted the ball into Brazil’s net once again.

After the match, the Daily Mirror praised this “Arsenal connection” and wrote on its front page: “Arsenal won the World Cup!”

Petit’s goal also personally overturned his own childhood prediction: the score in the World Cup final against Brazil was not 2–0, but 3–0.

### Zidane lifted the World Cup trophy high.

The match ended with France winning 3–0, claiming the World Cup for the first time in history.

It is worth mentioning that before the tournament, France head coach Jacquet had been heavily questioned by the media.

L’Équipe portrayed him as a self-righteous physical education teacher who only knew theory, lacked intelligence, imagination, and style, played conservative football, and would be unable to lead France to success.

These comments hurt Jacquet deeply. He responded by saying that “certain media outlets” were made up of “thugs, incompetents, dishonest and irresponsible people.”

After France won the World Cup, L’Équipe’s editor-in-chief Jérôme Bureau publicly admitted the mistake:

“Although we had deep doubts about Jacquet just a few weeks ago, today we sincerely congratulate him. We hope he can hear these good wishes.”

Jacquet did not forgive them. He replied:

“Some parts of the media shamelessly lied. I will never forgive them. I have only contempt for these people.”

France’s title also owed something to Ronaldo’s complete lack of impact in the final. He was dazed throughout the match and created little threat.

However, thanks to his excellent performances in the other matches, Ronaldo was still named the best player of the tournament.

After the final, some people claimed with hindsight that Brazil might have been better off if Zagallo had not allowed Ronaldo to play.

There were also conspiracy theories claiming that Brazil and Ronaldo’s sponsor Nike pressured Zagallo into starting him, and that Zagallo eventually gave in.

But this claim has never been supported by any solid evidence.

Edmundo, whose starting place was taken by Ronaldo in the final, firmly believed in this conspiracy theory. Many years later, when recalling the incident, he said:

“Nike staff were on standby 24 hours a day, just like members of the coaching staff. Their power was very strong. That is all I can say.”

After the match, one photo spread widely: Ronaldo standing on the pitch with a look of deep disappointment, wearing not only the silver runner-up medal around his neck, but also his Nike boots.

Some people saw this behavior as something done at Nike’s instruction.

### Ronaldo had Nike boots hanging around his neck.

Ronaldo later reflected:

“I played. Maybe I affected the whole team… In any case, I had a responsibility to my country. I did not want to miss the final. I respected my own honor and believed I was capable of playing. Of course, this was not one of the best matches of my career, but I still fulfilled my duty.”

Team doctor Toledo had cleared Ronaldo to play and was later heavily criticized.

But Toledo defended himself:

“Imagine this: if I had stopped him from playing and Brazil had lost, I would have had to go live in the North Pole!”

Ronaldo’s strange illness before the final became one of football history’s famous mysteries. No one can be 100 percent certain what caused it.

Apart from Bruno Caru’s later explanation that Ronaldo had compressed his carotid body while lying down watching the race, and Carlos’s initial suggestion of an epileptic seizure, there have been many other theories.

Some said Ronaldo had already been suffering from depression, but the team doctor did not dare give him medication because of doping risks.

Some said the team doctor had injected medicine into Ronaldo’s knee, causing an allergic reaction.

Some said Ronaldo’s girlfriend at the time was having an affair with a Brazilian journalist, and that Ronaldo had a mental breakdown after finding out.

Some even claimed that Brazil, following the words of then-FIFA president Sepp Blatter, deliberately used the incident to “sell” the World Cup to France.

Of course, all of these claims are mainly speculation. There is no real evidence, and they are difficult to prove.

We can only be certain of one thing: the possibility that he had epilepsy is very small. Epileptic patients usually suffer repeated episodes, but Ronaldo never experienced anything similar again after that incident.

What exactly happened to Ronaldo before the 1998 World Cup final remains full of unanswered questions to this day.

Four years later, Brazil once again reached the World Cup final, this time in Yokohama, Japan, against Germany.

On the day of the final, after lunch, Ronaldo did not even dare take an afternoon nap, because he was afraid that what had happened four years earlier might happen again.

This time, Ronaldo’s roommate was goalkeeper Dida.

Dida jokingly comforted him:

“Don’t worry. This time your roommate is not Carlos, so nothing like that will happen.”

To help Ronaldo relax, Dida took him to play golf for a while, and then Ronaldo took a short nap. As expected, nothing happened.

They went into the final in good spirits and eventually won it.
